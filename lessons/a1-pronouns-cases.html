Pronouns & Cases

Goal: I can use personal pronouns (mich, dir, ihm...) and possessives (mein, dein, sein...) correctly for every person.

One pronoun, three jobs

Before any rules — two real sentences. See how much you can already guess.

    Step 2 · Understand

    English already does this — just not for everything

    English changes I to me, and he to him, depending on the pronoun's job in the sentence. German does the same thing, but for every single person, not just a few: a pronoun has a nominative form (the subject — who's doing something), an accusative form (the direct object — who or what receives the action), and a dative form (the indirect object — to or for whom).

    You've already been using one piece of this without naming it — dir in Wie geht's dir?, back in the Greetings unit. This unit gives you the whole set, for every person, so dir stops being a special case and becomes one entry in a pattern you now recognize.

    Possessives (mein, dein, sein...) work exactly like ein and kein, which you already know from the Drinks and Questions & Negation units — one word per "owner," changing its ending the same way theirs did. One quirk to notice: ihr means both "her" and "their" — genuinely the same word — while capitalized Ihr means formal "your." Same sound, different word, only the capital letter tells them apart in writing.

    Step 2 · Understand

    The everyday past tense — and one more bracket

    Was hast du gestern gemacht? ("What did you do yesterday?") is how Germans actually talk about the past in conversation — not a formal or literary tense, the normal one. It's built the same way as the last two units: haben or sein, conjugated in the usual spot, plus a past participle pushed to the very end of the sentence — the same bracket that held a separable prefix in Daily Life and a whole infinitive in Modal Verbs, now holding a participle.

    haben or sein? Most verbs use haben. sein is for verbs of motion or a change of state — gehen (go), aufstehen (get up), and a handful of others. A rough test: if you could ask "wohin?" ("to where?") about the verb, it's probably sein.

    Forming the participle follows one of four patterns: regular verbs add ge- and -t (machen → gemacht); irregular verbs add ge- and -en, often with a vowel change (essen → gegessen) — these have to be memorized per verb, the same way English "eat → eaten" isn't predictable either; -ieren verbs skip ge- entirely (studieren → studiert); and separable verbs put ge- between the prefix and the stem (aufstehen → aufgestanden).

    Step 2 · Understand

    Why German has two words for "you"

    English uses you for everyone — a close friend, a stranger, your professor, all the same word. German (like French, Spanish, and many other languages) splits this into two: du for people you know well — friends, family, fellow students, children — and Sie for people you don't: strangers, professors, officials, most adults you're meeting for the first time.

    This isn't just grammar — using du with the wrong person can come across as overly familiar. When in doubt (especially early on, as a new student), Sie is the safer choice. Most people will tell you if they'd rather you switch to du.

    The next step separates two independent questions: what time is it? and who are you talking to?

    Step 3 · Learn the pattern

    Two questions, not one long list

    Every German greeting answers one or both of these questions. Learn the two questions, and the "list" mostly writes itself.

    The greetings and farewells

    Question 1: What time is it?

    This mostly decides your arrival greeting.

    Question 2: Who are you talking to?

    This decides your pronoun and, usually, your farewell.
